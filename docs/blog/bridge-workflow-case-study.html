How I Built a Cross-Platform Lead Funnel Using Zapier, n8n & Make

A real-world case study in platform orchestration

The Situation

A mid-sized real estate agency in the Philippines approached me with a familiar problem: their leads were leaking. They were running Facebook Ads campaigns that generated 200+ inquiries per month, but by the time their sales team followed up, most prospects had already gone cold. The bottleneck was not lead generation -- it was what happened after the lead came in.

Their existing process was entirely manual. A VA would check the Facebook Lead Ads dashboard every few hours, copy lead details into a Google Sheet, then message the sales team on Viber. High-value leads and lukewarm inquiries got the same treatment. There was no scoring, no automatic routing, and no CRM integration. They needed a system that could capture leads instantly, qualify them using AI, and route them to the right sales rep -- all without anyone lifting a finger.

The catch? No single automation platform could handle all of this optimally. After evaluating the requirements, I proposed a bridge architecture that uses three platforms in concert, each playing to its strengths.

The Architecture Decision

Rather than forcing one platform to do everything -- and hitting its limits -- I designed a pipeline where each tool handles the stage it is genuinely best at. The data flows through webhooks, creating a seamless chain the end user never sees.

Facebook Lead Ads
    ↓ (Zapier captures)
Zapier: Capture & Forward
    ↓ (webhook to n8n)
n8n: AI Qualification (GPT-4o-mini)
    ↓ (scored leads via HTTP)
Make: CRM Routing & BI Dashboard
    ├→ Hot Leads: HubSpot + Calendar + Slack
    └→ Nurture: HubSpot + Mailchimp

Each arrow in that diagram represents a webhook call. The entire pipeline executes in under 8 seconds from the moment a prospect submits a Facebook form to the moment the right sales rep gets a Slack notification with the lead score and recommended talking points.

Why Each Platform

The decision to use three platforms was not about complexity for its own sake. Each platform was chosen because it genuinely excels at a specific layer of the pipeline. Trying to replicate the same capabilities in a single tool would have meant higher costs, worse performance, or both.

Zapier -- Lead Capture Layer

Zapier has the best native Facebook Lead Ads trigger on the market. It fires instantly when a new lead comes in -- no polling delay, no complex OAuth setup. The Zap does exactly two things: it receives the lead and immediately posts the raw data to an n8n webhook. Keeping Zapier's role minimal means we stay well within the free tier's task limits. The agency's marketing team was already familiar with Zapier, so there was zero training overhead for this layer.

n8n -- AI Qualification Layer

n8n is where the intelligence lives. A Code node preprocesses the lead data, then an HTTP Request node calls OpenAI's GPT-4o-mini API with a custom prompt that scores the lead on three dimensions: budget readiness, timeline urgency, and property-type match. The model returns a structured JSON object with a score from 0 to 100 and a short summary. I chose n8n here because it gives me full control over the prompt engineering, retry logic, and JSON parsing -- things that are painful in Zapier and limited in Make.

Make -- CRM Routing Layer

Make's Router module is the star of this stage. Based on the AI score, the workflow splits into parallel branches. Hot leads (score 80+) get created in HubSpot as high-priority contacts, a Google Calendar event is booked for the assigned agent, and a formatted Slack message hits the sales channel. Leads scoring 40-79 enter a nurture sequence via Mailchimp. Leads below 40 are logged but not actively pursued. Make handles this fan-out pattern more elegantly than either Zapier or n8n because its visual router executes all branches simultaneously.

Cost Comparison

One of the most compelling arguments for the bridge architecture is cost. Here is what the numbers look like for processing approximately 200 leads per month:

Setup Monthly Cost
All-Zapier (estimated) ~$200/mo
Bridge Architecture ~$50/mo

The all-Zapier estimate accounts for multi-step Zaps with AI add-ons, premium app connections, and the task volume required. With the bridge approach, Zapier handles only the trigger (free tier or minimal plan), n8n runs self-hosted on a $5/mo VPS, and Make operates within its free tier for the routing volume. The only variable cost is OpenAI API usage, which runs about $3-5/mo for 200 leads with GPT-4o-mini.

The Results

  • 75% cost reduction compared to an all-Zapier implementation
  • 8-second end-to-end processing from form submission to sales notification
  • 92% AI qualification accuracy validated against manual scoring over 30 days
  • 3x faster follow-up for hot leads compared to the old manual process
  • Zero missed leads -- every submission is captured and processed automatically

The agency reported a measurable increase in conversion rates within the first month. Their sales team stopped wasting time on unqualified inquiries and could focus entirely on high-intent prospects. The Mailchimp nurture sequence also started converting cold leads that would have previously been ignored entirely.

Key Technical Decisions

Several technical choices were critical to making this architecture reliable in production:

Webhook authentication. Each webhook endpoint uses a shared secret passed in a custom header. The receiving platform validates this token before processing the payload. This prevents unauthorized triggers and ensures data integrity across the bridge points.

AI scoring thresholds. I spent two weeks calibrating the scoring prompt with the client's sales team. We analyzed 150 historical leads to define what "hot," "warm," and "cold" meant for their specific market segment. The prompt includes examples of each tier so GPT-4o-mini can pattern-match effectively.

Error handling at each bridge point. Every webhook call includes retry logic with exponential backoff. If n8n is temporarily down, Zapier retries the webhook up to 3 times. If Make fails to process a scored lead, n8n queues the payload and retries on a 5-minute schedule. A dead-letter Google Sheet catches anything that fails all retries, and a daily summary email alerts the team if any leads got stuck.

Idempotency. Each lead is assigned a unique ID at the Zapier stage. Downstream platforms check this ID before creating records, preventing duplicate CRM entries if a webhook fires twice. This is a detail most automation builders overlook, and it has saved this client from data quality headaches.

When to Use This Pattern

Bridge architecture is not always the right call. It adds complexity. But it makes sense in these scenarios:

  • You need AI processing that goes beyond simple text extraction -- custom scoring, summarization, or classification that requires prompt engineering and structured outputs.
  • You need parallel fan-out routing where a single input triggers multiple downstream actions simultaneously, each with different logic.
  • Cost is a constraint and you want to avoid premium tiers on a single platform by distributing work across free or low-cost tiers on multiple platforms.
  • Different stakeholders own different tools. If marketing manages Zapier and engineering manages n8n, a bridge architecture lets each team work in their preferred environment.
  • You are hitting platform-specific limits such as Zapier's lack of code flexibility, Make's limited AI integrations, or n8n's smaller app connector library.
  • You want graceful degradation. If one platform goes down, the others continue operating. Queued webhooks get processed once the failing service recovers.

Final Thoughts

The automation industry pushes you toward picking one platform and going all in. That works for simple workflows. But for real-world business processes that involve AI, complex routing, and cost optimization, the bridge pattern gives you the best of every world. The key is clean interfaces between platforms -- well-documented webhooks, shared authentication, idempotent processing, and robust error handling.
